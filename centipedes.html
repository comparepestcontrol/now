How to Get Rid of House Centipedes and Millipedes

House centipedes and millipedes are often lumped together because both have many legs, but they are not the same problem and they do not need the same response. A house centipede is a fast hunter with long, banded legs and a flattened body. It lives indoors or in damp indoor spaces and feeds on insects and spiders. A millipede is slower and more cylindrical, with two pairs of legs on most body segments. It lives in rotting leaves and mulch and usually comes indoors only when the yard is too wet or too dry.

If you treat every many-legged visitor as if it were the same insect, you can miss the cause. Centipedes often mean there is moisture and prey inside the house. Millipedes often mean the foundation is holding debris and water.

How to tell what you have

A house centipede runs quickly across a bathroom floor, basement wall, or bathtub and tries to hide in a crack. The legs are longer than the body is wide, and the insect can look larger than it is because of those legs. A millipede crawls slowly, may curl into a tight coil when disturbed, and is more often found near a door, garage, or ground-level window after rain.

Neither one is a typical threat to people, though a house centipede can bite if you try to catch it with your hands. Millipedes do not bite, but they can leave a stain or an odor if they are crushed. The larger issue is what brought them in.

If you regularly see house centipedes, look for other pests at the same time: silverfish, spiders, cockroaches, or other damp-room insects. The centipede is often feeding on those animals. If you mainly see millipedes after storms, look at mulch depth, leaf litter, and door seals first.

What you can do yourself

For house centipedes

Reduce moisture in bathrooms, basements, and crawl spaces. Fix leaks, run exhaust fans, and use a dehumidifier where the air stays damp. Vacuum the centipedes you see and empty the vacuum outside. Sticky monitors along basement and bathroom walls will show whether activity is falling.

Then work on their prey. Declutter storage rooms, vacuum unused corners, and follow the sanitation steps you would use for silverfish or cockroaches if those insects are also present. Seal gaps around pipes, windows, and door bottoms so both the hunters and the prey have fewer ways in. A wide insecticide spray is usually a weak answer if the room stays wet and full of insects.

For millipedes

Rake leaves and thinning vegetation away from the foundation. Keep mulch from sitting in a thick, wet layer against the siding. Improve drainage and downspouts so rain does not pool along the wall. Add door sweeps and repair gaps in the foundation or around ground-level vents. When a wave of millipedes comes in after a storm, vacuum or sweep them up and check that the door they used now seals.

Outdoor lighting is less of a factor for millipedes than it is for some flying insects, but a tightly sealed threshold still matters. If you use a product along the foundation, follow the label and treat it as a support for the cleanup, not as the main step.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a house centipede and a millipede?

A house centipede is fast, has long legs that fan out from a flattened body, and hunts insects indoors. A millipede is slower, more rounded, has two pairs of legs on most body segments, and feeds on decaying plant matter outdoors.

Are house centipedes dangerous?

They can bite if handled, but they usually run and hide. They also eat other insects, so a lot of house centipedes can mean the house has moisture and prey such as spiders or silverfish.

Why do millipedes come inside after rain?

Heavy rain or watering floods their hiding places in mulch and leaf litter. They wander in search of dry shelter and can enter through door gaps and foundation cracks.

When should I call a professional?

Call if house centipedes keep appearing after you have dried the house and reduced other insects, or if millipedes invade in large numbers that sealing and yard cleanup have not stopped.
