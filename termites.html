Signs of Termites in the House and What to Do

Termites can damage a house for a long time before anyone sees an insect. The signs are easy to miss if you do not know where to look, and they are easy to confuse with ants when a swarm appears at a window. If you suspect termites, treat it as a structural issue, not a simple cleanup job.

There are two broad groups that matter in many U.S. homes. Subterranean termites usually nest in soil and travel through mud tubes. Drywood termites live in the wood itself and leave small, dry pellets. The right treatment depends on which group you have, so identification comes first.

Signs of termites to look for

Mud tubes are one of the clearer signs of subterranean termites. These are pencil-wide tunnels of soil on foundation walls, piers, mudsills, or along pipes in a crawl space. The tubes let termites travel between soil and wood while staying hidden. If you break a short section and the tube is repaired in the next few days, activity is likely still underway.

Swarmers are another sign. These are winged termites that leave a colony to start a new one, often in spring or after rain. You may find them around windows and doors, or a pile of discarded wings on a sill, in a spider web, or on a garage floor. Wood that sounds hollow when you tap it, dark or bubbling paint, tight doors or windows, and thin galleries in damaged trim are also worth a closer look.

Drywood termites may leave pellets that look like fine sawdust or salt grains near a window frame, attic beam, or piece of furniture. You will not always see mud tubes with this group. In any case, do not ignore a single indoor swarm. Even if the winged insects die quickly, the colony that produced them may still be in the structure or the soil beside it.

How termites differ from ants, and what you can check

Flying ants are often mistaken for termites. A termite swarmer has a straight antenna, a broad waist, and two pairs of wings that are nearly equal in length. An ant has an elbowed antenna, a pinched waist, and front wings that are clearly larger than the hind wings. If you can collect a specimen or a clear photo, keep it for a technician. Do not rely on memory after you have vacuumed them up.

You can also walk the outside of the house and the crawl space, if you have one and it is safe to enter. Look for wood that touches soil, such as a porch post, stair stringer, or siding flare. Check for leaks, clogged gutters, and poor drainage that keep the foundation wet. Move firewood and lumber away from the house. These steps do not remove an active colony, but they make the property less inviting and help a technician see the structure clearly.

Do not start breaking into finished walls or soaking random sections of wood with a store spray. That can hide signs a professional needs, and it rarely reaches a subterranean colony in the soil.

When to call a professional

Call a licensed termite or pest control provider as soon as you see mud tubes, piles of wings, damaged wood, or a swarm indoors. A technician can confirm the species, inspect the foundation, crawl space, attic, and interior wood, and explain treatment options. Those options vary. Some jobs use liquid soil treatment, bait stations, or a combination. Drywood problems may need a localized treatment or a whole-structure method. The right choice depends on the inspection, not on a product you pick in advance.

Ask whether the inspection will be written, which areas were not accessible, and what monitoring or follow-up is included. Confirm licensing and insurance. If you are buying or selling a home, share any report you already have, but do not assume an old report still reflects the house.

Frequently asked questions

What are the most common signs of termites in a house?

Look for mud tubes on foundation walls, discarded wings on windowsills, wood that sounds hollow, and swarming insects indoors in spring or after rain. Pencil-thin galleries in wood and bubbling paint can also appear.

How can I tell termites from flying ants?

Termite swarmers have straight antennae, a broad waist, and two pairs of wings that are about the same size. Flying ants have elbowed antennae, a narrow waist, and front wings that are larger than the hind wings.

Can I treat termites myself?

A homeowner can fix moisture problems and keep wood from touching soil, but a confirmed termite infestation usually needs a licensed professional. Hidden damage can spread while store treatments only reach a small area.

When should I call a professional about termites?

Call as soon as you see mud tubes, piles of wings, damaged wood, or a swarm indoors. Early inspection is more useful than waiting to see if the signs go away.
